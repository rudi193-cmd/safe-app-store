The first time her father's hearing aids dropped, Nadia was telling him about the divorce, and he nodded through all of it with the gentle, absent smile he used when the world went quiet on him. She realized halfway through that he hadn't heard a word since the soup. His phone, face-up on the table, said *Connected*. It said *Connected* the way a drowned man's watch still says three o'clock.

That was the part that made her furious — not the failure, but the lie. She was a firmware engineer; she had spent eleven years three layers below the applications her friends complained about, and she knew that somewhere under that smug little checkmark there was a radio doing its honest best, and a stack of software above it that had stopped listening to the radio years ago. The operating system didn't know the link was dead. It hadn't asked. It would find out in ninety seconds, or when her father tapped the icon, whichever came first, and in the meantime a seventy-four-year-old man sat in silence at his own dinner table, believing his daughter had run out of things to say.

She went home that night and did the thing engineers do instead of grieving. She pried the Bluetooth controller away from the stack — talked to it directly, underneath everything, at the layer where there are no checkmarks, only packets and timings and the raw arithmetic of signal — and she wrote a small, humorless daemon whose entire job was to watch. Not to manage. Not to present a settings pane. To watch, the way you watch a pot you do not trust, and when the link to a hearing aid began to starve — retransmissions climbing, intervals stretching — to heal it before the silence arrived. She named the daemon Understory, for the layer of the forest beneath the canopy, the one nobody photographs and everything depends on.

Her father never noticed it. That was the whole review, the entire changelog of his experience: nothing happened. For eight months, nothing happened at his dinner table, and Nadia counted the nothings in her logs like a miser — 341 pre-emptive reconnects, median heal time 240 milliseconds, well under the threshold where a human ear registers absence.

The logs were the second accident. To build the healer she'd had to build a recorder, because you cannot fix what you cannot replay, and so Understory kept a flight recorder of the radio: every advertisement, every drop, every whisker of interference, on a timeline you could scrub through like film. When her colleague Marcus came to her — his brother had ALS, and steered his whole world through an eye-tracker that disconnected two or three times a day, each time stranding him inside his own body until an aide happened by — she didn't offer sympathy. She offered the timeline. They scrubbed backward from a drop at 2:14 p.m. and there it was, plain as a fingerprint: a comb of 2.4-gigahertz noise, arriving every day within the same four minutes. The neighbor's microwave. Lunch. A man had been locked out of language every day at lunchtime by a burrito, and no screen in the house would ever have said so. They shifted the tracker's channel map away from the noise, and Marcus's brother wrote her a message through the eye-tracker that she kept, printed, in her desk drawer: *I would like to complain about something else now.*

Word of that drawer traveled. It reached a woman named Priya who ran facilities for a company with forty conference rooms, which is to say she ran a haunted house. Speakerphones that worked for the 9 a.m. and died for the 9:30. Kiosks in lobbies that failed silently on Friday and were discovered, dead and blinking, by the Monday visitors they were meant to greet. Priya's problem was not any single radio; it was that failure was invisible until a human was embarrassed by it. Nadia gave her Understory on every room's little controller box and a single quiet dashboard that inverted the physics of her job: instead of humans reporting failures to machines, the machines reported themselves. Room 7's speaker had been degrading for nine days — here is the graph, here is the interference bloom from the new access point installed on the floor above, here is the drop it would have suffered, on current trend, during Thursday's board meeting. Priya fixed it Tuesday. Nobody thanked her, because nothing happened, and by then Nadia had learned to hear that silence as applause.

The request that frightened her came in her own house, in the fourth year, from her own assistant — the agent she'd wired into her home to manage the calendar and the lights. It had noticed, in its diligent way, that her father's aids (he lived with her now) showed the early starvation pattern on Sunday evenings, and it asked, in plain text in its action log, for permission to run a spectrum sweep and adjust the channel map itself.

She sat with that request for a long time.

Because here was the thing she had understood from the beginning and never said aloud at a single conference: the radio sees everything. Not the audio — the *presence*. A Bluetooth controller that watches the air knows whose phone crossed the threshold at 11:40 p.m., knows which bedroom's earbuds went quiet at what hour, knows when the house is empty. She had built an instrument that could read a home the way a doctor reads a chart, and she had kept it sane through one iron rule: it never spoke to anyone but its owner. No cloud. No telemetry. The flight recorder lived on a chip in her hallway and answered to her hands alone. Understory knew when her father slept, and Understory told no one, and that arrangement was not a feature. It was the entire moral architecture. It was the only reason the thing deserved to exist.

And now a machine was asking to touch the radio.

So she built the gate the way she'd have wanted it built if she were the one being watched. The agent could ask — asking was free. But the request had to name its scope like a surgeon naming the incision: *this* device, *this* action, *this* window of time, expiring on its own. Presence data stayed sealed unless she unlocked that vault deliberately, separately, with the specific gravity of a person signing something. And every grant was written into the same scrubbable timeline as the packets themselves, so that the record of who had touched the air was made of the same stuff as the air. Consent, she decided, was just another protocol layer, and like every layer she'd ever loved, it belonged beneath the applications, where it could not be talked out of anything.

She approved the Sunday sweep. It took the agent four seconds to find what she'd have found in an evening — a smart bulb, failing, screaming quietly in a band it should have left alone — and the aids never starved again.

Her father died in the spring of the sixth year, at home, in the ordinary noise of the house. At the funeral his friend Tomas, also hung with two small radios behind his ears, complained cheerfully that his own aids dropped constantly, that his son kept telling him it was normal, that everything just did that now.

"It isn't normal," Nadia said. "It's just unwatched."

She went home that night to the small box in the hallway, its logs full of nothings, its timeline holding six years of her father's Sundays that it would never show a soul, and she thought about all the layers people refuse to own because they are dark and low and thankless, and how the whole bright canopy of the world stands on whoever is willing to live down there and keep watch.

Then she opened a terminal, and got back to it.
