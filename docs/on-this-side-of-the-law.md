# On This Side of the Law

*A paper on the gap between legality and morality — and on what it costs to close
part of it with a machine.*

---

> On this side of the law
> On that side of the law
> Who is right? Who is wrong?
> Who is weak? Who is strong?
> Who is for and who's against the law?

Five questions in five lines, and the verse's real work is done by putting them
in the same breath. *Right/wrong*, *weak/strong*, and *for/against* are three
different questions, and law's oldest rhetorical trick is to let them collapse
into one. This paper pulls them apart, argues that only the third is genuinely
about sides, and then does something the philosophy literature usually can't:
points at a working legal order small enough to read in an afternoon — this
repository's own promotion gate — and asks what it actually costs to make a
rule *mean* something.

---

## I. Right and wrong is not the law's question

Law asks whether a thing is *lawful*. Morality asks whether a person owes
another person better. These overlap most of the time — murder, theft, fraud —
and the overlap is so wide and so load-bearing that people mistake it for
identity. It isn't.

The counterexamples are not exotic. The Fugitive Slave Act of 1850 was law, and
obeying it meant returning a human being to bondage. The Nuremberg Laws of 1935
were law, promulgated, published, applied by trained judges in functioning
courts. Apartheid's pass laws were law. Sheltering a Jewish family in occupied
Amsterdam in 1942 was a crime, and the people who did it were criminals in
exactly the sense the word ordinarily carries. If legality and morality were the
same predicate, those sentences would be contradictions. They are not
contradictions; they are the ordinary condition of the twentieth century.

This is the **separability thesis**, and it is the core of legal positivism from
Bentham and Austin through Hart: *the existence of a law is one thing, its merit
or demerit another.* Positivism is routinely slandered as the doctrine that
whatever is legal is fine. It is close to the opposite. Its whole point is to
keep the two questions apart **so that the second one can still be asked.** If
you define law such that an unjust law isn't really law, you have not abolished
the injustice — you have abolished the vocabulary for naming it. Hart's argument
was that the citizen facing a monstrous statute is better served by "this is
law, and it is too evil to obey" than by "this is not law," because the first
sentence tells the truth about the danger they are in and still leaves the moral
conclusion intact.

Positivism is also not the claim that law and morality never touch. Hart himself
conceded a **minimum content of natural law**: given that humans are vulnerable,
roughly equal in strength, limited in altruism, and living amid scarcity, any
legal system that survives at all will contain some restrictions on violence,
some property rules, some enforcement. Not because justice demands it, but
because a system without them has no subjects left. That is a floor, not a
ceiling, and it is a very low floor — it is fully satisfied by a regime that
protects its members and predates on everyone else.

## II. Hart and Fuller: the grudge informer

The canonical collision is the 1958 exchange in the *Harvard Law Review* between
H. L. A. Hart and Lon Fuller. Its test case: a German woman who, wanting rid of
her husband, denounced him to the authorities for insulting Hitler — a capital
offence under statutes then in force. He was sentenced, survived, and after the
war she was prosecuted. But under what theory? She had reported a real offence
under a real statute.

Hart and Fuller agreed she should be punished and disagreed about the honest
route. **Fuller** argued the Nazi statutes were so far from law's inner
requirements that they never earned the name — that legality is not merely a
label but a craft with standards, and a system failing them produces something
other than law. **Hart** answered that the statutes *were* law, that pretending
otherwise is a comfortable fiction, and that if the postwar state wanted to
punish her it should pass a **frankly retroactive statute** and accept the
stain. Two evils — letting her go, or punishing under a backdated law — and the
adult move is to name the one you're choosing rather than dress it as no evil at
all.

That is the durable lesson, and it is more about intellectual honesty than about
jurisprudence: *when both options are bad, the fiction that one of them is clean
is a third and worse option.*

Two footnotes worth keeping. First, the grudge-informer case as Hart received it
appears to have been misreported; later scholarship (Pappe, 1960) found the
actual court reasoned on narrower grounds than the "unjust laws are not law"
line the debate hung on. The philosophical dispute survives the correction, but
it is a useful reminder of how often a famous case is a story about a case.
Second, Fuller's positive account — the **eight ways to fail to make law**, from
*The Morality of Law* (1964) — turned out to be his more lasting contribution,
and we will need it in Part VI. A system fails, he argued, if it lacks general
rules, or fails to publish them, or abuses retroactivity, or writes them
unintelligibly, or contradicts itself, or demands the impossible, or changes
them so often that no one can orient, or — the last and most important —
**fails to make official action congruent with the declared rule.** Fuller
called this law's *internal morality*: not a theory of good ends, but of what
any rule must be like to function as a rule at all. It is procedural. It is
also, notably, the part of this whole literature that a computer can check.

## III. Radbruch's formula, and the guards at the wall

Gustav Radbruch, a positivist before 1933, wrote in 1946 the formula that bears
his name: positive law holds even when unjust — but where the conflict with
justice reaches an **intolerable** degree, the statute yields; and where a rule
deliberately betrays the equality that is justice's core, it lacks the nature of
law entirely.

That is not, as it's often taught, a stampede back to natural law. It is a
narrow emergency exit, deliberately built too small to walk through casually.
The presumption still favors the statute. Only *intolerable* conflict defeats
it.

The formula's afterlife is instructive because it was actually used. After
reunification, Germany prosecuted the *Mauerschützen* — border guards who shot
people crossing the Berlin Wall — and the officials who set the shooting policy.
The defence was the strongest one available: we followed the law then in force,
and punishing us now is retroactive criminal law. The Federal Court of Justice
invoked Radbruch: a rule authorising the killing of unarmed people for trying to
leave their country is intolerably unjust and cannot furnish a justification. In
*Streletz, Kessler and Krenz v. Germany* (2001), the European Court of Human
Rights upheld the convictions and found no breach of the ban on retroactive
punishment.

Note precisely what that holding is, and isn't. Nobody claimed the guards should
have divined a hidden higher law. The reasoning ran nearer to Fuller: the GDR's
*own written law* protected life and permitted force only proportionately, and
the shoot-to-kill practice was a matter of orders and internal policy that
diverged from the published text. The conviction rests less on "morality
overrides law" than on **incongruence between the declared rule and official
action** — Fuller's eighth failure, doing the work that grand natural-law
rhetoric gets credit for. Which is a pattern: when courts must condemn a prior
regime, they reach for the *procedural* failure far more often than the
substantive one, because the procedural failure can be demonstrated.

## IV. Nuremberg: the moral choice was in fact possible

The related question — not *was the law valid* but *does obedience excuse* — got
its answer at Nuremberg. Article 8 of the Charter: acting on superior orders
does not free a defendant from responsibility, though it may mitigate
punishment. The ILC's 1950 restatement, Principle IV, adds the qualification
that carries the whole moral weight: obedience is no defence **"provided a moral
choice was in fact possible to him."**

That clause is the hinge, and it is easy to miss. It concedes that duress is
real, that hierarchy is real, that a conscript at gunpoint is differently
situated from a minister at a conference table. It does not accept that the
existence of an order settles the question. What it establishes is a floor
beneath legality: there are acts for which "it was lawful where I stood" is not
an answer, and knowing this in advance is the point. It converts obedience from
a safe harbour into a **decision**, which is exactly what a functionary needs it
to not be.

## V. Weak and strong: the sharpest line in the verse

The third question — *who is weak, who is strong* — is where the poem gets
dangerous, because it names that someone **wrote** the thing.

Law is not weather. It has authors, drafting histories, lobbyists, committee
chairs, and a budget. Someone decided which harms get a statute and which get
called misfortune; which get a specialist enforcement agency and which get a
civil remedy nobody can afford to pursue. Thrasymachus said in Book I of the
*Republic* that justice is the advantage of the stronger, and the cheap
refutation is that he's a cynic. The expensive one is to check.

Check enforcement asymmetry. In the United States, credible estimates put
employer wage theft — unpaid minimum wage, stolen overtime, off-the-clock work —
in the same order of magnitude as *all* reported property crime losses combined;
one widely cited study found minimum-wage violations alone running to billions
annually across the ten most populous states. Set that beside the enforcement
apparatus: tens of thousands of police agencies for the theft that runs one
direction, a few hundred federal wage-and-hour investigators for the theft that
runs the other. Both are illegal. Only one is *policed*. That difference is not
in the statute books; it is in the appropriations, and appropriations are
written by whoever is in the room.

Anatole France put the structural version best in 1894: the law, in its majestic
equality, forbids rich and poor alike to sleep under bridges, to beg in the
streets, and to steal bread. Formal equality applied to unequal positions is a
machine for producing unequal outcomes while producing paperwork that says
otherwise.

So the honest reformulation of the verse's third line is not *is this legal* but
**whose interests did this become legal to protect, and who was not in the room
when it was drafted?** That question is empirical. It has answers. It is also
the question most reliably deflected by the response "but it's the law," which
is why the deflection is so popular.

One guardrail, though, or this collapses into its own cheap cynicism. "The law
serves power" is a claim about *tendency*, not about every provision. Habeas
corpus, the exclusionary rule, whistleblower protections, the wage-and-hour
statute itself — these are law too, and they exist because someone fought power
and won a rule out of it. Law is contested ground, not simply an instrument. If
it were purely an instrument the powerful would not spend so much to change it.
The correct posture is neither reverence nor dismissal but **audit**: ask of any
given rule who it binds, who it exempts, and who pays to enforce it.

## VI. For and against: the trap, and the way through

The last line — *who is for and who's against* — offers two camps, and the
positions worth having are in neither.

There is a serious case for obeying laws you believe unjust. Legitimacy is a
commons. A society where everyone exempts themselves by private conscience is
not a moral society; it is a society where power has simply been redistributed
to whoever is most confident. Socrates makes this case in the *Crito* with the
Laws of Athens speaking in their own voice, and then drinks the hemlock rather
than escape a verdict he considers wrong. That is not servility. It is the
recognition that he has taken the city's benefits for seventy years and cannot
now claim its rules apply to everyone else.

There is an equally serious case against. Antigone buries her brother against
Creon's explicit edict, invoking the unwritten and unfailing laws that are not
of today or yesterday. Aquinas: an unjust law is a corruption of law. Both
traditions are old, both are coherent, and neither has ever defeated the other,
which should tell us the question is not the kind that gets settled.

The strongest position is a third thing, and it belongs to the practitioners
rather than the philosophers. Martin Luther King Jr., writing from the
Birmingham jail in 1963, distinguishes just from unjust law with a test that is
about *structure*, not sentiment: an unjust law is one a majority compels a
minority to obey but does not make binding on itself. And then the crucial
sentence about method — one who breaks an unjust law must do so **openly**,
lovingly, and with a willingness to accept the penalty. Rawls formalises the
same move: civil disobedience is a public, non-violent, conscientious political
act contrary to law, undertaken *within the limits of fidelity to law itself*.

The openness is not decoration. It is the entire argument. Breaking a law in
secret and obeying without ever asking are more alike than either party would
admit: **neither has paid anything, and neither has said anything.** Accepting
the penalty is what converts a violation into a claim — it demonstrates that you
are not exempting yourself from the shared order but indicting one rule from
inside it, and it puts the cost of the indictment on you rather than on
everyone. That is why the sit-in is an argument and the bribe is not, though
both are illegal.

So the sides are not *for* and *against*. They are: those who take the law's
presumption seriously enough to pay for departing from it, and those who never
priced it at all — in either direction.

## VII. The mechanism half: what it costs to mean a rule

Everything above is about legal orders too large to see. This repository
contains one small enough to read, and it is a better teacher than most of the
literature — because a rule you can execute is a rule that cannot quietly not
mean what it says.

`stores/promote_check.py` governs one crossing: a build in the shared `apps/`
playground becoming a standing SAFE app. Read as jurisprudence, it makes five
commitments, each of which has a name in the tradition.

**1. A rule of recognition, written down and executable.** Hart's answer to
"what makes something law here?" is a social rule the officials share about
which rules count. In most systems that rule is tacit, contested, and reverse-
engineered from behaviour. Here it is a file. The bar in `stores/README.md` is
prose; `promote_check.py` is that prose made mechanical — *"turns the promotion
bar from prose into a fail-closed check."* Anyone can read what promotion
requires before attempting it, which is Fuller's second desideratum
(promulgation) satisfied unusually literally.

**2. The presumption is withheld, not granted.** `apps/` is *contested by
default* — nothing in the playground is trusted until it is promoted. This is
the inversion of the ordinary civic presumption, and correctly so: the
presumption of innocence protects a person against a state with overwhelming
power, whereas here the asymmetry runs the other way, and the thing seeking
admission is code seeking the fleet's trust. Match the presumption to who holds
the power, not to habit.

**3. Fail-closed, including on what cannot be verified.** The gate refuses
anything failing a criterion *and* anything it cannot check. That second clause
is the interesting one: it places the burden of proof on the proposer and
declines to read silence as consent. Most institutional failures are not a rule
being broken; they are a rule having no defined behaviour for the unverified
case, and the unverified case being waved through because someone was in a
hurry.

**4. Proposing and ratifying never rest in the same hand.** `verified_by` must
differ from `author`, and the gate refuses to write the record even if every
other check somehow passed. This is separation of powers at the smallest
possible scale, and the design note names precisely what it buys: it *"turns
§0.2 from a thing a person remembers into a mechanism."* That sentence is the
whole subject of this paper compressed to a line. A norm held only in memory is
enforced against the tired, the rushed, and the conflicted exactly when
enforcement matters most. Mechanising it is not distrust of the person. It is
recognising that the person will one day be the author, at 2 a.m., with a
deadline.

**5. The verdict leaves a mark.** A passing run writes
`stores/{major}/promoted/<app_id>.json` — the verdict, every individual gate
result, who verified it, when — *"so the passage leaves a mark instead of
scrolling past in a terminal."* This is Fuller's eighth and hardest
requirement: **congruence between the declared rule and official action.** The
requirement most legal systems fail, because verifying it demands records that
someone has an interest in not keeping. Here the record is a side effect of
passing, which is the only durable way to get it.

Run Fuller's eight desiderata against this gate and it scores better than most
sovereign states: rules are general, published, prospective, intelligible,
non-contradictory (`CLAUDE.md` §5/§8 and `stores/README.md` were deliberately
brought into one vocabulary *"after the two used to disagree"* — a codification
of exactly the kind that legal systems undertake when their sources drift),
compliable, stable, and congruent by construction.

### The line the machine cannot cross, and why it is marked

The most honest thing in the file is its two-kind taxonomy. Gates are
**`[M]` mechanical** — verified directly, by running tests, walking the AST,
resolving symbols — or **`[A]` attested** — asserted by a human, with the script
enforcing only the assertion's *shape*, failing closed if it is absent.

That distinction is the paper's real conclusion. Every rule-governed system
eventually meets a property it cannot check: whether the tests are *meaningful*,
whether the extraction was *honest*, whether the verifier actually looked. You
can respond three ways. Pretend the unmechanizable properties don't matter —
which is how metrics eat institutions, and how a system drifts until it values
only what it can count. Pretend you've checked them anyway — which is theatre,
and worse than nothing because it consumes the trust that real checking would
earn. Or **draw the line explicitly and put a name on the far side of it.**

The third is what an `[A]` gate does. It does not eliminate the moral judgement;
it *locates* it, attaches a person to it, and records that attachment in a file
that outlives the terminal session. Judgement doesn't vanish at the limits of
mechanism. It gets a signature.

That is as good an answer as I know to the verse. You cannot automate right and
wrong. You *can* automate the conditions under which right and wrong get
decided by someone who is named, who is not the same person as the proposer, who
had the rule in front of them before they started, and whose verdict is written
down where it can be checked later. Procedural morality is not a substitute for
the substantive kind. It is the thing that keeps the substantive question from
being quietly skipped — which, empirically, is how it is usually lost. Not
overruled. Skipped.

## VIII. Where it lands

The law deserves a presumption, not a surrender. It is the accumulated,
imperfect, heavily-lobbied answer to how strangers share a place without killing
each other, and treating every disagreement with it as licence dissolves the
thing that makes disagreement survivable in the first place.

But a presumption is defeasible, and four questions tell you when to test it:

1. **Who does this rule bind, and who does it exempt?** (King's test.)
2. **Who was not in the room when it was written?** (The verse's third line.)
3. **Is official action congruent with the declared rule** — or is the
   published text a cover for a practice? (Fuller's eighth; the Wall guards.)
4. **Was a moral choice in fact possible?** (Nuremberg Principle IV — asked of
   yourself, prospectively, not of others afterwards.)

And when the presumption is defeated, the question stops being which side of
the law you are on. It becomes: *what are you willing to pay to say so out
loud?* On this side or that side, the people worth listening to are the ones
who answered that question with something other than nothing.
