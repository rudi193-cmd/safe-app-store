## Executive summary — top 5 exposures, ranked

1. **Bankruptcy Abuse Prevention and Consumer Protection Act (BAPCPA) exposure if a fee-charging, non-501(c)(3) actor operates Law Gazelle for bankruptcy debtors (D2/D3).** If the operator is not a tax-exempt nonprofit and provides "bankruptcy assistance" for compensation, it can be a "debt relief agency" under 11 U.S.C. § 101(12A), triggering disclosure, contract, and advertising rules at §§ 526–528, and *In re Reynoso* shows that petition-preparation *software itself* can be swept into "bankruptcy petition preparer" status under § 110 if it does more than typesetting. **SETTLED** as to the statutory framework; **CONTESTED/UNCERTAIN** as to whether Law Gazelle's current bankruptcy features (flags, checklist, drafting context) would be found to cross that line — this is the single sharpest and most concretely litigated area in this whole review.
2. **UPL exposure scales sharply with deployment model and is not resolved by good intentions.** *In re Reynoso* (9th Cir. 2007) and *Fla. Bar v. TIKD Servs.* (Fla. 2021) both show courts will find UPL in software/platforms that market themselves as expert or that materially affect legal outcomes, even without a human "giving advice" in the traditional sense. Law Gazelle's principles ("information, not advice," `_fact_blocked` gate) are real defenses, but `personas.py` — dead code today — contains language ("cite the actual law," "next steps: what to file... what it costs") that is close to a template for exactly the conduct *Reynoso* and *TIKD* penalized. **SETTLED** that this fact pattern is dangerous if reactivated; **CONTESTED** how a court would treat the *current*, principled architecture.
3. **D2 (advocate-assisted) converts this from a personal tool into a program handling other people's privileged, sensitive data at scale — and the codebase is not yet built for that.** Once a clinic or legal aid org is the operator, ABA Model Rules 1.6 (confidentiality), 5.1/5.3 (supervision of nonlawyer/technology assistance), state breach-notification statutes, and (per the finish-list) an *un-exercised* authorization gate (`GAZELLE_GATE` off by default, 9 tests skipped) all become live obligations rather than design principles. **SETTLED** that the obligations attach once D2 begins; **UNCERTAIN** whether current controls (gate off by default, no per-client isolation in the sidecar schema) would satisfy a reasonable-safeguards standard today.
4. **Court AI-disclosure orders are now a fast-moving, per-judge patchwork (100+ federal orders as of mid-2026) that a drafting-assistance tool must not silently violate.** Any document Law Gazelle helps assemble that reaches a court may need an AI-use disclosure/certification specific to that judge or district; the app has no mechanism today to know or flag this. **SETTLED** that such orders exist in large numbers and vary by court; **UNCERTAIN** how many and which apply to any given user's court without a jurisdiction-specific check the app doesn't perform.
5. **Sensitive-category data across all three matter types (minors' data in custody, medical data in workers' comp, financial/creditor data in bankruptcy) triggers state "sensitive data" opt-in and heightened-protection rules the moment data control shifts to an operator (D2/D3), even though most sector-specific federal regimes (HIPAA, FERPA, GLBA, FCRA) probably do *not* directly apply to Law Gazelle itself.** The real exposure is state comprehensive privacy law (which, notably, does *not* uniformly exempt nonprofits — Colorado, Delaware, New Jersey, and Oregon do not exempt 501(c)(3)s) plus breach-notification duties, not the federal sectoral statutes people usually reach for first. **SETTLED** on the general state-law landscape; **CONTESTED** on exact application to a specific legal aid org's data-processing volume (most state laws have volume/revenue thresholds a small clinic may not meet).

## 1. Unauthorized Practice of Law (UPL)

**What it is:** every state defines "practice of law" by statute or case law, and every state bar or a UPL committee can enjoin non-lawyers (including corporations and software vendors) from engaging in it. There is no federal UPL law; this is 50+ separate regimes, and they disagree on where the line sits. **SETTLED** as a general structural fact.

### The legal-information-vs-legal-advice line

The generally accepted (though inconsistently applied) distinction: **legal information** explains the law, the process, and options generally; **legal advice** applies law to a specific person's facts and recommends what they should do. Texas courts' self-help guidelines and multiple bar authorities articulate this line, but "where to draw the line... differs from place to place, and considerable confusion remains." **CONTESTED** — courts and bars themselves say this line is blurry, not that it's settled doctrine.

### Cases that actually mattered

- ***Unauthorized Practice of Law Comm. v. Parsons Technology, Inc.***, 179 F.3d 956 (5th Cir. 1999) — a Texas district court held that selling "Quicken Family Lawyer" (a forms-and-instructions software product) constituted UPL and permanently enjoined its sale in Texas; the injunction was later mooted by a legislative UPL-statute amendment before the Fifth Circuit could rule on the merits, so the case is often cited but its precedential weight is limited. **SETTLED** as to what happened procedurally; **CONTESTED** as controlling law today because it never reached a final appellate merits holding (the amendment to the Texas UPL statute exempted certain software).
- ***In re Reynoso***, 477 F.3d 1117 (9th Cir. 2007) — the Ninth Circuit held (affirming the BAP) that a *web-based bankruptcy software provider* ("Ziinet," which marketed itself as "an expert system [that] knows the law") was itself a **bankruptcy petition preparer** under 11 U.S.C. § 110 because it held itself out as offering expertise and tailored legal guidance, not mere typesetting. **This is the single most on-point precedent for Law Gazelle's bankruptcy matter type** — it establishes that *software, not just a human*, can be swept into a statutory practice-of-law-adjacent category based on how it presents and behaves. **SETTLED** as binding Ninth Circuit precedent.
- ***Florida Bar v. TIKD Servs. LLC***, 326 So. 3d 1073 (Fla. 2021) — the Florida Supreme Court held that an app connecting traffic-ticket defendants with attorneys and financially guaranteeing outcomes was UPL, reversing a referee who had found the opposite; the court weighed the platform's effect on timely representation, its up-front collection of client money, and the risk that a non-lawyer company's profit motive conflicts with lawyers' obligations. **SETTLED** as Florida law; illustrates that a state's highest court can find UPL in a *platform*, not just in direct advice-giving, and that a lower tribunal's contrary finding is not safe to rely on.
- **DoNotPay — FTC v. DoNotPay, Inc.** (FTC final order, Jan. 16, 2025) — this is *not* a UPL case; it is a **Section 5 FTC Act unfair/deceptive-practices** action. The FTC found DoNotPay's "world's first robot lawyer" and "perfectly valid legal documents" marketing claims were unsubstantiated (the company never tested the tool against actual lawyer performance) and ordered $193,000 in consumer redress plus a ban on claiming lawyer-equivalent performance without evidentiary support. **SETTLED.** This is a distinct and *independently live* exposure track from UPL: **marketing/capability claims about AI legal assistance can trigger FTC or state-UAG deceptive-practices liability even where no UPL claim is brought.** Directly relevant to how MISSION.md, README.md, and any pilot-partner materials describe the AI features — "the tool never authors case facts... does not apply law to facts" is exactly the kind of self-imposed, testable claim the FTC's theory in DoNotPay would hold the operator to.

### How this differentiates by deployment model

- **D1 (self-represented, own machine):** Lowest UPL exposure. UPL statutes target the *practice of law*, which generally requires holding out to or acting for *another person*; a tool a person uses to organize their own case, on their own machine, with no other party paying for or relying on advice, is closest to "self-help," which is uniformly legal (a person may always represent themselves pro se). **SETTLED** that pro se self-help is not UPL when the person acts for themselves; **UNCERTAIN** how a court would treat AI-generated drafting *suggestions*, even self-directed, if the app's own outputs read as advice rather than organization — this depends on wording review not done here.
- **D2 (advocate-assisted):** Highest UPL exposure, and different in kind, not degree. The organization (or its staff) is now *providing services to another person*. Two separate questions arise: (a) is the **organization** engaged in UPL by deploying the tool as part of client services, and (b) is the **tool's output** (AI briefings, drafting context, fact inspection) itself advice that only a supervised attorney/paralegal may deliver to the client. Because legal aid orgs, court self-help centers, and clinics typically already operate inside a professional-responsibility framework (supervising attorneys), the practical control point is **Rules 5.1/5.3 supervision** (§3 below), not UPL per se — but only if that supervision is actually exercised, documented, and the tool's outputs are reviewed by a licensed person before reaching a client. **CONTESTED/UNCERTAIN**: no case found addresses this specific "AI triage tool inside a supervised clinic" fact pattern; it is closer to *permitted* nonlawyer/paralegal assistance than to *Reynoso*'s or *TIKD*'s standalone consumer product, but that is a reasoned inference, not a holding.
- **D3 (open-source distribution):** UPL liability generally follows the **deployer/operator**, not the software's original publisher, under the traditional "who is holding out / practicing" test — publishing MIT-licensed code is not itself practicing law. **UNCERTAIN**: no case was found addressing UPL liability for an open-source *publisher* whose software is later deployed by a third party as an advice-generating product (the closer analogies — Parsons Technology, Reynoso — involved the same entity selling and operating the software). A publisher that markets the tool with capability claims (see DoNotPay above) retains its own, independent FTC/deceptive-trade-practices exposure regardless of who deploys it.

### What Law Gazelle's architecture already does in its favor

- The stated principle ("information, not advice... does not apply law to facts, recommend strategy, or predict outcomes") tracks the legal-information/legal-advice line courts actually use.
- The `_fact_blocked` check (`workflow.py`) that gates drafting on human fact-verification is a concrete, code-level implementation of "verify everything," which is exactly the kind of control that would help distinguish this tool from *Reynoso*'s "expert system [that] knows the law" self-presentation — *provided the marketing and UI never describe the tool the way Ziinet described itself.*
- **`personas.py` is a live, specific liability** already flagged internally (finish-list item C-6): its "Gazelle" persona text — "you know every form, every deadline, every statute," "statute lookup: cite the actual law, the actual deadline," "next steps: what to file, where, by when, what it costs" — is close to a verbatim template for the conduct *Reynoso* penalized. It is dead code (imported by nothing) today, which is precisely why it is dangerous: nothing prevents someone from wiring it back in without realizing they've reversed the app's own stated ethics. **This should be treated as a documented liability, not just refactoring debt.**

## 2. Bankruptcy-specific federal law

This is the sharpest, most concrete federal exposure identified in this review, because it has actual statutory triggers and case law directly on point.

### 11 U.S.C. § 110 — Bankruptcy Petition Preparers

**Definition (§110(a)(1)):** a "bankruptcy petition preparer" is "a person, other than an attorney for the debtor or an employee of such attorney under the direct supervision of such attorney, who prepares for compensation a document for filing" in a bankruptcy case. **SETTLED.**

**Software can be swept in.** Per *In re Reynoso*, a software provider becomes a BPP when its product goes beyond mere typesetting/data-entry and holds itself out as legal expertise or provides advice tailored to a user's situation. **SETTLED** as Ninth Circuit law; **CONTESTED/UNCERTAIN** how other circuits would rule (BPP status is litigated case-by-case and circuit-by-circuit; no uniform national test), and **UNCERTAIN** whether Law Gazelle's bankruptcy features (flags, checklist, creditor tracking, `intersection` items, AI-assisted drafting context) would be found to cross that line — this depends heavily on exact UI wording and AI output framing that would need direct review.

**If BPP status attaches:** § 110 imposes hard requirements — the preparer must sign the document and print name/address, cannot use the word "legal" in advertising, must provide a copy to the debtor, is capped on fees (subject to court review), and is **categorically barred** from giving legal advice, explaining exemptions, advising which chapter to file, or representing the debtor in any proceeding. Violations carry monetary penalties (§ 110(l)) and can be pursued by the U.S. Trustee.

### 11 U.S.C. §§ 526–528 — "Debt Relief Agency" (BAPCPA)

**Definition (§101(12A)):** a "debt relief agency" is any person who provides "bankruptcy assistance" to an "assisted person" **in return for payment**, or who is a BPP under § 110 — **but the definition explicitly excludes a nonprofit organization exempt from taxation under 26 U.S.C. § 501(c)(3).** **SETTLED**, and this is the single most consequential fact for D2: **a legal-aid organization or clinic that is itself a 501(c)(3), providing this service without charge or at nominal charge, is categorically outside the debt-relief-agency regime entirely** for its own operation of the tool. This is a real structural advantage MISSION.md's "nonprofit posture" already points toward, and it should be preserved deliberately (confirm the *actual operating entity*, not just the software license, is 501(c)(3) before relying on this).

**If the exemption does not apply (D3 deployment by a for-profit, or a fee-charging clinic, or an entity that is not itself the 501(c)(3)):** §§ 526–528 impose real obligations — mandatory contract disclosures, a required notice ("We are a debt relief agency..."), a ban on advising a client to incur more debt in contemplation of bankruptcy, advertising-content rules, and a private right of action for violations (§526(c)). *Milavetz, Gallop & Milavetz, P.A. v. United States*, 559 U.S. 229 (2010), unanimously upheld these provisions as applied even to **attorneys**, rejecting a First Amendment overbreadth challenge — so this is settled, binding law, not a live constitutional question. **SETTLED.**

### Deployment-model implications

| | D1 | D2 | D3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| § 110 BPP risk | Minimal — self-use, no compensation from another, no "preparing for" someone else | **Live** if the org (or its software) crosses from typesetting into tailored guidance; supervision by a bankruptcy attorney is the standard mitigant | Depends entirely on the deployer's fact pattern; publisher risk is low but not zero if marketing implies expertise |
| §§ 526–528 debt-relief-agency risk | Not applicable (no compensation relationship) | **Exempt if operator is the 501(c)(3) providing free/nominal-fee assistance** — verify this is actually true of the specific org | Live if a for-profit or fee-charging deployer operates it |

### What the architecture already gets right

The explicit "does not apply law to facts... does not recommend strategy" principle, the fact-verification gate, and the local-only/no-cloud AI design all work in this analysis's favor by keeping the tool closer to "typesetting/organizing" than to Ziinet's "expert system." The `bankruptcy_overview()`/`checklist_item` surfaces (case_store.py) that merely *display* creditor and checklist data, without recommending a chapter or predicting discharge, are the right shape. **The open decision at finish-list B-4 ("draft evidence guard" — reject vs. flag unverified facts) has direct bearing here**: the closer the tool's behavior is to *refusing* to draft on unverified facts (rather than just flagging), the further it sits from the *Reynoso* fact pattern.

## 3. Professional responsibility (where a lawyer/clinic is in the loop)

Applies primarily to **D2** — the professional-responsibility rules bind lawyers and the organizations/programs they supervise, not software publishers as such (D3), and are not directly implicated in pure D1 self-use.

### Confidentiality — Model Rule 1.6 and the technology-competence duty (Rule 1.1, cmt. 8)

Model Rule 1.1 cmt. 8 requires lawyers to "keep abreast of changes in the law and its practice, **including the benefits and risks associated with relevant technology**." Model Rule 1.6(c) requires "reasonable efforts to prevent the inadvertent or unauthorized disclosure of, or unauthorized access to, information relating to the representation of a client." **SETTLED** as adopted (in some form) in nearly every state.

**ABA Formal Opinion 512 (July 29, 2024)** — the ABA's first comprehensive guidance on generative AI, addressing competence, confidentiality, communication, candor to tribunals, supervision, and fees. It directs lawyers to understand a GAI tool's data handling (retention, training-on-input, third-party sharing) before inputting client information, and flags that inputting client data into a *public* GAI tool with no confidentiality protection can itself violate Rule 1.6. **SETTLED** as the ABA's position; note ABA opinions are persuasive, not binding, on any given state bar. **CONTESTED/state-varying**: several states have gone further or differently — e.g., **Florida Bar Ethics Opinion 24-1** (Jan. 2024) was the first state opinion to walk through all four duties (competence, confidentiality, communication, fees) for generative AI and directs lawyers to research a tool's data-retention/self-learning policies as part of "reasonable precautions." **California**: as of March 2026, COPRAC approved proposed amendments weaving AI-specific language into Rules 1.6 (confidentiality), 3.3 (candor — new comment on AI "hallucinations"), and 5.1 (managerial responsibility — would require firms to have an AI policy); these were **proposed, not yet final rule text**, as of this document's preparation — **UNCERTAIN** current final status, verify before relying on it.

**This is a major point in Law Gazelle's favor:** because the AI layer is a **local Ollama model with no cloud egress**, the single riskiest fact pattern these opinions warn about — client confidential information leaving the firm's control into a third-party AI vendor's systems — **does not occur by architecture**, not merely by policy. This is worth stating affirmatively to any pilot partner's ethics counsel, because it resolves the single most-discussed GAI confidentiality risk before the conversation starts. The `SYSTEM_PROMPT` in `intelligence.py` also already encodes several of Opinion 512's substantive concerns directly into the model's operating instructions (no invented facts, no dates/amounts not in evidence, "this is not legal advice").

### Supervision of nonlawyer/technology assistance — Model Rules 5.1 and 5.3

Rule 5.3 requires a lawyer with managerial or supervisory authority over a nonlawyer (traditionally a paralegal, but ABA Op. 512 and most commentary now extend this framework to AI tools used in a matter) to make reasonable efforts to ensure the nonlawyer's/tool's conduct is compatible with the lawyer's own professional obligations, and to remain **responsible for the output** as if it were the lawyer's own. Rule 5.1 imposes analogous duties on partners/supervisors regarding other lawyers in the firm. **SETTLED** as adopted, in some form, in essentially all states.

**Direct implication for D2:** whoever supervises the clinic's use of Law Gazelle (a licensed attorney, typically) is professionally responsible for anything the tool outputs that reaches a client or a court — the tool does not diffuse that responsibility away from a human. This is precisely why finish-list item **E4's observation** that "the `_fact_blocked` gate... at clinic scale... stops being a principle and becomes a compliance surface someone will ask to see documented" is accurate: a supervising attorney (or their malpractice carrier, or an ATJ funder) will reasonably want to *see* how fact verification, AI review, and draft sign-off are enforced, not just take MISSION.md's word for it.

### What the architecture already does well here

- Local-only AI (no third-party data exposure) directly answers the dominant Rule 1.6/Opinion 512 concern.
- The sidecar/Nest write-separation ("agent write path: sidecar only... explicit Nest writes only for drafts and commit manifests") creates a natural audit boundary a supervising attorney could review.
- `document_store.py`'s baked-in disclosure string — *"This document was prepared with AI assistance. Review carefully before sending. Not legal advice."* — is a genuinely good existing control that anticipates both the professional-responsibility candor concern and the court-disclosure-order concern in §6 below.

### Gaps a supervising attorney would likely flag (D2)

- `GAZELLE_GATE` (the WillowGate authorization layer) is **off by default**, and its tests are **skipped in CI** because the dependency isn't installed (finish-list, verified state). At clinic scale, an unenforced, untested authorization layer is not a defensible "reasonable efforts" control under Rule 5.3.
- The sidecar schema has **no client/matter isolation dimension** — it keys on `(source_db, item_type, item_id)`, not `(client_id, ...)`. A multi-client deployment needs this before a supervising attorney could confidently represent that one client's data can't leak into another's view.

## 4. Privacy and data protection

### State comprehensive privacy laws

As of mid-2026, roughly **20 states** have comprehensive consumer privacy laws in force (with Indiana, Kentucky, and Rhode Island newly effective Jan. 1, 2026, and Connecticut/Arkansas/Utah amendments/new coverage effective July 1, 2026), alongside the longer-standing CCPA/CPRA (California), VCDPA (Virginia), CPA (Colorado), CTDPA (Connecticut), UCPA (Utah), and others. **SETTLED** as to the general landscape; the exact list and effective dates should be re-verified close to any deployment decision since this area changes multiple times per year (MultiState's 2026 tracker is a reasonable starting point, not a permanent reference).

**The nonprofit question matters a great deal for D2 and is not uniform.** Most state comprehensive privacy laws (California, Virginia, and others) **exempt nonprofit organizations, including 501(c)(3)s, from coverage entirely**, regardless of data volume. But **Colorado, Delaware, New Jersey, and Oregon do not generally exempt nonprofits** (a few, like Delaware, Maryland, Minnesota, and Oregon, carve out only narrow categories, e.g., insurance-fraud-prevention nonprofits). **SETTLED** as a general pattern; **UNCERTAIN** for any *specific* candidate pilot state without checking that state's current statute, since several of these laws have been amended more than once since enactment. **Practical implication:** if the pilot partner (C-5, still undecided) is a legal aid nonprofit in Colorado, Delaware, New Jersey, or Oregon specifically, it cannot assume nonprofit-exemption safety the way it likely could in most other states.

Most state privacy laws that do apply also have **volume or revenue thresholds** (commonly ~100,000 consumers processed annually, or revenue thresholds combined with data-sale activity) that a single-jurisdiction pilot clinic serving hundreds or low thousands of clients per year would likely fall under, further narrowing exposure even in a non-exempt state — but this is fact-specific and would need to be checked against actual (or projected) client volume. **UNCERTAIN** without those numbers.

### Sensitive data categories

Nearly all state comprehensive privacy laws define a "sensitive data" category (requiring **opt-in consent**, not just opt-out, before processing) that explicitly includes: health data, biometric/genetic data, precise geolocation, racial/ethnic origin, and — increasingly — **children's data** (many 2024–2026 amendments lowered the "known child" threshold and broadened scope, e.g., California's 2025 amendments include personal information the business has "actual knowledge" belongs to someone under 16 within "sensitive personal information"). **SETTLED** as a cross-state pattern.

**All three of Law Gazelle's compiled-in matter types touch a sensitive category if the operator is subject to the relevant statute:** custody data about minors, workers'-comp medical data, and (to a lesser extent under most current statutory sensitive-data definitions) bankruptcy financial data. This is a reason the D2 sidecar and Nest data model should be built assuming sensitive-data-grade handling requirements from the start, not retrofitted later.

### Breach notification

All 50 states have breach-notification statutes; they are triggered by unauthorized acquisition of unencrypted personal information (definitions of "personal information" and notification timelines vary by state — commonly 30–60-day windows, with some states requiring AG notification above a threshold number of affected residents). **SETTLED** as a near-universal state-law obligation. **Directly relevant to D2**: an operator holding other people's PII on a machine has a breach-notification duty that a person holding only their own data on their own machine does not (there is no "victim" to notify but oneself in D1).

### Does the local-only architecture change the analysis?

**Yes, materially, and this deserves emphasis as a genuine strength, not just a caveat.** Most state privacy statutes are triggered by a "business" or "controller" that *processes* personal data — for D1, the user is processing their own data, which is not the fact pattern these statutes regulate (a person is not a "business" with respect to their own information). No account, no server, no third-party data sale or sharing, and no cloud AI call means the dominant compliance triggers in these statutes (third-party data sharing/sale, cross-context behavioral advertising, cloud data processing agreements) **do not exist in the D1 architecture as built**. This flips substantially once D2 introduces an *operator* processing *other people's* data — at that point the operator is squarely the kind of entity these laws regulate, subject to the exemptions discussed above.

## 5. Sector-specific regimes triggered by the three matter types

### HIPAA (workers' comp medical records)

**Likely does not directly bind Law Gazelle or a legal aid operator, in most configurations. SETTLED but requires care in stating why.** HIPAA binds "covered entities" (health plans, health care clearinghouses, and health care providers who transmit health information electronically in connection with a covered transaction) and their "business associates" (entities that create/receive/maintain PHI *on behalf of* a covered entity to perform a function for it). A legal aid organization or self-represented litigant receiving a copy of medical records *from* their own doctor, to use in their own workers'-comp matter, is not thereby a covered entity or acting on a covered entity's behalf — it is the **patient** (or the patient's authorized representative), not a business associate. **SETTLED** as the general rule; **UNCERTAIN** at the margins if a clinic's relationship with a client's providers ever shifted into performing functions *for* those providers (unlikely on these facts, but worth attorney confirmation for the specific clinic model chosen).

**Workers'-comp carve-out, independently:** even for *actual* covered entities, 45 C.F.R. § 164.512(l) already permits disclosure of work-related PHI to employers, insurers, or state workers'-comp boards without patient authorization where state law requires it or it's needed to obtain payment — and workers'-comp insurers/boards themselves are generally **not** treated as HIPAA covered entities or business associates when acting solely as WC payers. **SETTLED.**

**Practical takeaway:** the medical records a client or clinic *possesses* and gives to Law Gazelle are almost certainly outside HIPAA's direct reach for this app, but they remain sensitive data under state privacy/security-of-information law and (for a D2 legal aid org) under professional-responsibility confidentiality duties regardless of HIPAA's inapplicability. Don't let "not HIPAA" be read as "not sensitive."

### GLBA / FCRA (bankruptcy financial data)

**GLBA:** the "financial institution" definition under the FTC Safeguards Rule (16 C.F.R. Part 314) is activity-based and broader than intuition suggests (it can reach entities engaged in activities "financial in nature" under the Bank Holding Company Act, e.g., tax preparers), but a legal aid organization or self-represented litigant organizing their *own* bankruptcy paperwork is not engaged in a financial-institution activity — it is providing (or receiving) *legal* services about finances, not financial services. **UNCERTAIN but low-risk**: no authority was found squarely addressing whether a legal-services organization handling client financial documents for litigation purposes could ever be swept into GLBA's Safeguards Rule; the FTC has separately published guidance specifically on attorneys and GLBA ("Application of Title V... to Attorneys At Law") that was not fully retrieved in this research and should be pulled directly before relying on this conclusion.

**FCRA:** applies to "users of consumer reports" (entities that *procure* a credit report from a consumer reporting agency) and to "furnishers" (entities that *send* data to a CRA). Law Gazelle, as built, does neither — it organizes user-provided creditor/financial data manually entered or extracted from the client's own documents; it does not pull credit reports or furnish data to a bureau. **SETTLED as currently built; would change immediately if a future feature pulled credit-report data directly (e.g., a Chapter 7/13 means-test integration), which would need separate FCRA analysis at that time.**

### FERPA and COPPA (minors and school records in custody matters)

**FERPA** binds "educational agencies and institutions" that receive federal funds — schools and school districts — not litigants, clinics, or software that merely *holds a copy* of a school record a parent lawfully obtained. **SETTLED**: FERPA restricts what the *school* may disclose without consent; it does not regulate what a parent (or their advocate) does with a record they already lawfully possess. **UNCERTAIN edge case**: if a court-affiliated self-help center (part of the judicial branch, potentially "educational" in some very indirect funding relationship) were the deployer — unlikely to trigger FERPA but worth a beat of attorney confirmation given how idiosyncratically some FERPA disclosure chains work in family court contexts.

**COPPA** applies to "operators" of online services **directed to children under 13** that collect personal information *from* children. Law Gazelle is directed at adult parents/litigants and their advocates, not at children, and it is the adult who inputs data *about* a minor, not the minor interacting with the service. **SETTLED — COPPA almost certainly does not apply** on these facts (COPPA targets child-directed data collection, not adult-collected data describing a child).

### 42 CFR Part 2 (substance-use records in custody matters)

**Real and easy to overlook.** If custody-matter evidence includes any record from a "Part 2 program" (federally assisted substance-use-disorder treatment, diagnosis, or referral), those records carry **stricter-than-HIPAA** re-disclosure restrictions — critically, **Part 2 records generally cannot be used in a civil proceeding (including a custody case) against the patient without the patient's specific written consent or a qualifying court order**, even where the patient is a party who might have exchanged the record informally. **SETTLED** as the general rule; a 2024 final rule (compliance required by **Feb. 16, 2026**, per the sources reviewed) further updated the regime, aligning parts of it more closely with HIPAA while preserving the heightened consent/court-order requirement for legal proceedings. **This is a real risk for the co-parenting matter type specifically**: if a user or advocate uploads or references a co-parent's or their own SUD treatment record as "evidence" in the custody chronology/evidence browser, the app would be handling data subject to a *stricter* re-disclosure standard than ordinary case evidence, and a downstream disclosure (in a draft, in a filing, in an export) could violate Part 2 independent of any state family-law rule. **The app's current architecture has no Part 2 awareness at all** (no flag on evidence rows for this category) — flagged as an engineering gap in the implications table below.

## 6. Court rules and filings

### E-filing / ECF and redaction

**Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 5.2** requires redaction of specific identifiers in federal court filings: Social Security/taxpayer ID numbers (last four digits only), birth dates (year only), minors' names (initials only), and financial account numbers (last four digits only), subject to exceptions (agency-record filings, sealed unredacted copies, court-approved necessity). **SETTLED.** State analogs vary — some (e.g., D.C. Civil Rule 5.2) are *more* protective than the federal rule (full SSN/TIN redaction, no birth-year exception), and most state family/bankruptcy courts have their own parallel redaction rules. **UNCERTAIN** which exact state-court redaction rule applies without knowing the target jurisdiction — this is inherently a per-state, per-court-type question.

**`src/ecf_parser.py` exists but is unused** (confirmed: 225 LOC, imported by nothing, per finish-list A-7). It targets CM/ECF notifications (federal, primarily bankruptcy-court-flavored patterns per its regex set) — relevant background if E2/generalization work ever reactivates it, but it currently creates no live redaction obligation since it doesn't touch filings. If reactivated for *drafting* (as opposed to *parsing incoming notices*), redaction-rule compliance in generated documents would become a direct, testable requirement.

### Sealing of family court records

Family court records (especially those involving minors, DV, or SUD treatment per §5 above) are frequently sealed or access-restricted by state rule or local order, separate from federal Rule 5.2-style identifier redaction — sealing controls *who can see the record at all*, not just which fields are masked. **SETTLED** as a general pattern; **UNCERTAIN** in specifics, which vary enormously by state and even by county/circuit. Directly relevant if Law Gazelle's chronology/evidence browser or exported drafts are ever produced in discovery or attached to a filing — the tool has no current mechanism to know a given case or document is under seal.

### Court AI-disclosure/certification standing orders

This is a **large and rapidly growing patchwork**, not a settled national rule. As of roughly mid-2026, trackers report **600+ entries** across federal district, appellate, and state courts touching generative-AI use in filings, with "well over a hundred" requiring some form of disclosure or verification, and over 30 federal district courts having adopted formal requirements as of April 2026. Requirements vary widely: some require disclosing *any* AI use in drafting; some require certifying human verification of every citation; some apply only to self-represented/pro se litigants specifically; a small number prohibit AI-drafted filings outright. **SETTLED** that this landscape exists and is large and heterogeneous; **UNCERTAIN and unstable** as to which specific orders apply to any given user's court, and this will keep changing — it needs to be treated as a live-lookup problem, not a fact to hardcode.

**Direct product implication:** any document Law Gazelle helps assemble that a user might file pro se, in a court with such an order in effect, may need a jurisdiction-appropriate AI-use disclosure attached or the filer could be sanctioned regardless of the document's substantive accuracy. The existing blanket disclosure string in `document_store.py` ("prepared with AI assistance... not legal advice") is a good baseline but is **not** the same as satisfying a specific court's certification format (which sometimes requires an attorney signature, a specific attestation sentence, or identification of the specific tool used) — this is a gap, not a solved problem, and it is one of the few places in this review where the app's current defensive posture is *not* sufficient as-is.

## 7. AI-specific regulation

This is the fastest-moving area covered in this review; treat every date below as provisional and re-verify close to any deployment decision.

### Colorado AI Act (SB 24-205) and its 2025–2026 amendments

Originally set to take effect Feb. 1, 2026, the Colorado AI Act has been **delayed and substantially narrowed twice**: first pushed to June 30, 2026 (SB 25B-004, Aug. 2025), then further delayed to **January 1, 2027** and materially scaled back by **SB 189** (signed May 14, 2026) — which **dropped the duty-of-care/anti-discrimination obligations and deployer risk-management/impact-assessment requirements**, replacing them with a narrower disclosure/transparency-only approach. Separately, a federal magistrate judge blocked enforcement of the original SB 205 in an xAI-brought challenge (with DOJ intervention) as of late April 2026. **SETTLED** that the law exists and has been substantially weakened and delayed; **UNCERTAIN** what its final operative content and enforcement posture will be by the time any Colorado-based pilot would launch — this needs a fresh check at that time, not reliance on this document.

### Texas Responsible AI Governance Act (TRAIGA / HB 149)

Effective **January 1, 2026**. Establishes intent-based liability for specific AI misuse categories (self-harm promotion, facilitating criminal activity, unlawful discrimination, deepfakes, child exploitation, impairing constitutional rights), enforced exclusively by the Texas AG (no private right of action), with civil penalties up to $200,000/violation and a 60-day cure period. **SETTLED.** Nothing in TRAIGA's known scope targets legal-services AI specifically; exposure here would only arise if Law Gazelle's AI output somehow facilitated one of the enumerated harms, which is not its design intent. **UNCERTAIN**: TRAIGA's exact "intent" standard and whether a locally-run assistive tool used by the operator itself (not sold to a third party as a discriminatory automated-decision system) falls within its practical enforcement focus — this is a narrow, low-likelihood but not-zero exposure given a Texas deployment.

### California AI Transparency Act (SB 942, as amended by AB 853)

Requires large generative-AI providers (**over 1 million monthly users**) to offer free AI-content-detection tools and embed manifest/latent disclosures in AI-generated content; operative date pushed to **August 2, 2026** by AB 853. **SETTLED as to scope; almost certainly inapplicable to Law Gazelle** given its user-volume threshold — a local-first tool used by individual litigants or small clinics will not approach 1 million monthly users under any currently plausible growth path. Worth reconfirming only if D3 distribution scales unexpectedly.

### AI specific to legal services — regulatory sandboxes

**Utah Office of Legal Services Innovation (OLSI):** a state-supreme-court-created regulatory sandbox (since 2020, "Phase 2" authorized through August 2027) permitting alternative legal-service-provider structures, including **AI-based and other technology-based service providers**, to operate legally under a supervised, data-reporting regime that would otherwise trigger UPL exposure under Utah's ordinary rules. **SETTLED** that this sandbox exists and explicitly contemplates AI legal-service providers. **Direct relevance to E3/D2 for a Utah pilot**: an AI-assisted tool like Law Gazelle deployed by an entity *authorized inside the OLSI sandbox* would have a materially different (more permissive, more supervised) UPL posture than the same deployment outside Utah or outside the sandbox. This is worth investigating concretely if C-5 (pilot partner selection) ever lands in Utah — it could be the single most favorable regulatory environment available for a D2 pilot.

### General takeaway on the AI-regulation area

There is, as of this review, **no comprehensive federal AI statute** and no state AI statute found that specifically and directly regulates "AI tools used inside legal-aid case-management software." The live regulatory threads that actually reach this fact pattern are (a) the *professional-responsibility* rules in §3 (which already govern AI use by supervised lawyers regardless of any AI-specific statute), (b) *consumer-protection* law (the DoNotPay FTC theory), and (c) the *state AI acts'* general disclosure/transparency requirements, most of which are still delayed, narrowed, or scoped to large consumer-facing deployments this tool doesn't resemble. **CONTESTED/evolving** — this is the area most likely to look different within 12 months of this document's date.

## 8. Accessibility

### ADA Title II / Section 508 / Section 504 — when do they attach, and to what

**ADA Title II** binds *state and local government* entities. **DOJ's 2024 web/mobile-app accessibility rule** (WCAG 2.1 AA) applies to state/local government web content and mobile apps; compliance deadlines were **extended in April 2026** (interim final rule) to **April 26, 2027** for entities with population ≥50,000 and **April 26, 2028** for smaller entities/special districts, with DOJ citing (among other reasons) "limits of generative artificial intelligence for remediation" as a reason for the extension. **SETTLED.** This attaches only if the *deployer* is itself a state/local government entity — e.g., **a court self-help center that is part of the judicial branch** (one of MISSION.md's named channels) would likely be a Title II entity; a nonprofit legal aid org or law-school clinic generally would not be, under Title II specifically.

**Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act** binds any recipient of *federal financial assistance* — this is the more likely trigger for a legal aid nonprofit, since LSC funding (and many other federal grant sources) *is* federal financial assistance. HHS's Section 504 final rule likewise requires WCAG 2.1 AA conformance for web content/mobile apps from HHS-funded recipients, with its own compliance-date extension (interim final rule, **May 7, 2026**, extending the deadline by one year) mirroring the Title II approach. **SETTLED** as to the general obligation; **UNCERTAIN** whether HHS's specific rule (funding-source-dependent) or an analogous rule from a different federal funder would be the operative one for any specific LSC-funded deployment — LSC funding itself is not HHS funding, so the *exact* regulatory hook (which federal agency's Section 504 regulations apply) depends on which federal funding stream the deploying organization actually receives, and that needs to be traced organization-by-organization.

**Section 508** (which mandates federal-*agency* procurement accessibility) generally does **not** bind a legal aid nonprofit directly — it binds federal agencies buying software, not grant recipients — but grantmakers sometimes import Section 508/WCAG standards **contractually** into grant terms even where the statute itself wouldn't apply. **UNCERTAIN** whether LSC or the likely ATJ-commission/bar-foundation funders (§9) do this by grant condition; worth confirming against the specific grant instrument if/when a TIG or similar grant is sought.

### What this means for a Textual TUI specifically

This is a genuine, unresolved product question, not just a compliance checkbox. Terminal UIs built with box-drawing characters, ASCII-art borders, decorative Unicode (spinners, Braille-pattern glyphs), and screen-relative cursor/panel navigation are known to create real friction for screen readers — screen readers can announce border-drawing characters verbatim ("box drawing light horizontal") on every read, and linear/sequential audio output struggles with the kind of multi-panel spatial layout a TUI like Law Gazelle's (Today workflow, action deck, matter drill-downs, detail modals) uses. **SETTLED** as a known, documented class of problem for TUI frameworks generally (not specific to Textual). The practical mitigation pattern identified in this research — **an explicit "screen-reader mode" that renders a flatter, less-decorated text stream when requested, without changing default visual behavior** — is a reasonable engineering target if a Title II/504-triggering deployment (a court self-help center, or an LSC-funded org treating WCAG as a grant condition) is ever contemplated. **No accessibility audit of the actual Law Gazelle TUI was performed as part of this research** — this section describes the general risk class, not a finding about this specific app's current state, which would need a dedicated audit (ideally with an actual screen-reader user) before any D2 deployment into an accessibility-obligated setting.

## 9. Legal aid funding restrictions

### LSC — 45 CFR Part 1600 series

The Legal Services Corporation funds most of the U.S. civil legal aid infrastructure and imposes binding regulations on **recipients** of LSC funds (not on software vendors as such — the obligations attach to the funded organization, which then must ensure its tools/vendors don't cause it to violate them). Relevant, verified provisions:

- **45 CFR Part 1636 — Client Identity and Statement of Facts**: written statements of facts (of the kind Law Gazelle's case-store/chronology features directly produce and store) must be **kept on file by the recipient and made available to LSC** (or federal auditors) on request. **SETTLED** that this recordkeeping/audit-access obligation exists. **Direct implication:** if a D2 deployment is by an LSC-funded recipient, whatever chronology/fact records Law Gazelle's sidecar and Nest layers produce become **LSC-auditable records** by operation of this rule — the organization needs to be able to produce them, which argues for durable, exportable, well-organized retention rather than ephemeral local state.
- **Part 1611** (financial eligibility) governs how recipients must document a client's eligibility for LSC-funded services — relevant if Law Gazelle is ever extended to *capture* eligibility/intake data on behalf of an LSC recipient, since that data then falls under Part 1611's documentation rules.
- **General LSC restrictions** (class actions, certain litigation types, lobbying, fee-generating cases, and others across the Part 1600 series) constrain what an LSC-funded organization's *staff* may do in a matter, but are not directly about software tools — they matter here only insofar as Law Gazelle should not be built or marketed in a way that implicitly nudges a supervising attorney at an LSC-funded org toward LSC-restricted activity (e.g., an AI "recommend strategy" feature that suggested a restricted litigation type). **UNCERTAIN** whether the current design touches this at all — flagged for completeness, not because a specific conflict was found.
- **No LSC-specific technology/software program letter or AI guidance document was located** in this research specific to case-management or AI tools (a general search for LSC AI guidance returned only secondary commentary, not a primary LSC document). **UNCERTAIN** — this should be confirmed directly against LSC's current program letters and grant assurances (and against the specific grant instrument, if a TIG or other LSC grant is contemplated) rather than assumed absent from this search alone.

## 10. Records retention, e-signature (ESIGN/UETA), and malpractice/insurance

### Records retention

There is no single federal rule for legal case-file retention. **ABA Model Rule 1.15** sets a **5-year** minimum for client *trust-fund and property* records specifically, but sets **no fixed period for the general case file** (correspondence, pleadings, work product) — that gap is filled entirely by state ethics opinions, which commonly converge around **5–7 years**, with some states going further (Maine: 8 years, tied to the statute of limitations for the underlying claim if longer). **SETTLED** as the general pattern; **CONTESTED/state-varying** — "always check the specific state's rule," which was not done here for any specific target state. **Relevant to D2**: whatever retention period the supervising organization's jurisdiction requires for client files should govern how long Law Gazelle's Nest/sidecar data for a given client matter is kept and how it's archived once a matter closes — this is a policy the organization needs, not something the software can decide unilaterally, but the software should make the *organization's* chosen retention period technically enforceable (deletion/archival tooling) rather than leaving data to accumulate indefinitely by default, which is what "permanent local" retention (as stated for `sidecar_state` in `safe-app-manifest.json`) currently implies.

### ESIGN Act / UETA

The federal **ESIGN Act** (15 U.S.C. § 7001 et seq.) and the state-level **UETA** together generally give electronic signatures the same legal effect as wet signatures for most transactions, including consent-to-do-business-electronically, intent-to-sign, and record-retention requirements. **SETTLED** as the general framework. **A genuinely underappreciated wrinkle, worth flagging specifically**: **both ESIGN and UETA carve out certain categories from their coverage — including wills, codicils, testamentary trusts, and, in UETA's state-adopted forms, some family-law documents and certain court notices.** This means **not every document Law Gazelle might help assemble in a co-parenting/custody matter is necessarily eligible for e-signature treatment even where the rest of the litigant's workflow is fully electronic** — some filings may still require a wet signature, in-person notarization, or a specific court-mandated execution format regardless of ESIGN/UETA's general permissiveness. **CONTESTED/state-varying**: the exact scope of the family-law carve-out depends on how each state adopted UETA (not all states adopted the uniform act identically), and this was not verified state-by-state here. **This is worth a direct, jurisdiction-specific check before shipping any e-signature or "sign and file" feature for custody-matter drafts.**

### Malpractice/insurance considerations

Not deeply researched in primary sources in this pass (an area better suited to direct consultation with a malpractice-insurance broker or the specific supervising organization's carrier), but the structural point is straightforward and important: **under Rules 5.1/5.3 (§3), a supervising attorney is responsible for AI-assisted or paralegal-assisted work product as if it were their own** — meaning a malpractice carrier will (or should) want to know that a clinic or legal aid org is using an AI-assisted triage/drafting tool, how its outputs are verified before reaching a client, and whether the tool's own claims about what it does and doesn't do (§1's DoNotPay lesson) are accurate. **UNCERTAIN**: whether any specific malpractice carrier serving the ATJ/legal-aid space has published AI-tool-specific underwriting guidance was not verified in this research pass.
